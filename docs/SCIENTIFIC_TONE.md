# Scientific tone and evidence language

This guide is advisory. It is intended to improve precision without flattening the
author's voice.

> A supportive container, not a Procrustean bed.

## Evidence ladder

Use language that matches the strength of the evidence.

| Level | Appropriate wording | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Direct observation | “We observed…”, “We measured…”, “The analysis showed…” | A result directly reported by the study |
| Supported interpretation | “Our results provide evidence that…” | An interpretation supported by the reported results, not a proven fact |
| Compatible explanation | “The findings are consistent with…” | Multiple explanations may remain plausible |
| Preliminary indication | “This observation points towards…” | A hint that requires further testing |
| Weak indication | “The result suggests…” | A cautious interpretation |
| Untested explanation | “We hypothesise that…” | A proposed explanation, not a result |
| Future possibility | “This may enable…” | A potential application that remains to be demonstrated |

Avoid “proves” except for formal mathematical proof. Avoid treating “statistically
significant” as synonymous with important, causal, clinically meaningful, or
independently replicated.

## Neutral disagreement with literature

Do not strengthen a new contribution by diminishing prior work. Reviewers may be
authors of the studies being discussed, but the deeper reason is scientific fairness.

Prefer:

> Smith and colleagues reported increasing stiffness with degeneration in patients.
> Under the controlled ex vivo conditions used here, we observed the opposite trend.
> The difference may reflect variation in biological context, loading conditions,
> disease stage, or the measured structural parameter.

Avoid:

> Previous studies were wrong.
>
> Our method is clearly superior.
>
> Our work goes deeper than earlier research.

When justified, describe a contribution as complementary:

> Mueller and colleagues introduced XX, opening a route towards YY. Building on that
> framework, we investigated…

Narrative attribution must remain accurate. Do not replace the first author with a group
leader unless that person is an author and the wording faithfully represents the cited
contribution. Reviewer suggestions and citation credit are separate decisions.

## Results versus interpretation

Results should state what happened. Discussion should state what the authors think it
means.

Result:

> Mean stiffness decreased from \(12.1 \pm 1.8\) kPa to \(9.4 \pm 1.5\) kPa
> (\(n=18\), 95% CI for the mean difference 1.6–3.8 kPa).

Interpretation:

> Our results provide evidence that controlled degeneration reduced stiffness under
> these loading conditions.

Preliminary implication:

> This observation points towards a load-dependent alteration of the nucleus-pulposus
> microstructure.

## Comparative claims

Words such as “higher”, “lower”, “improved”, “reduced”, “stronger”, “unchanged”, and
“superior” should be supported by numbers, uncertainty, a defined comparison, and the
relevant statistical or qualitative basis.

## Limitations

A limitation section usually belongs near the end of the Discussion.

Constructive structure:

1. State the limitation clearly.
2. Explain its likely consequence.
3. State what remains supported.
4. Identify the next discriminating test.

Example:

> Although encouraging, this pilot study included a relatively small number of
> specimens. The confidence interval therefore remains wide and the result should not
> be generalised to clinical populations. Nevertheless, the consistency of the effect
> across repeated loading cycles supports independent replication in a larger series.
